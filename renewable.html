Renewable energy

Energy that refills itself faster than we use it

Coal and gas took millions of years to form and are burned once. Renewable sources are replaced continuously by sunlight, weather and plant growth. This page covers the four that matter most in a tropical climate, what each one is good at, and where each one struggles.

Introduction

The same job, four different methods

Every source on this page does one thing: it turns some form of natural energy into electricity. What separates them is where that energy comes from, how reliably it arrives, and how much land, water or money is needed to capture it.

No single source is best everywhere. Malaysia receives strong sunlight all year but has low average wind speeds, so a mix that works well in Denmark would perform poorly here. Choosing the right technology for the location is most of the engineering work.

The sources

Four ways to generate clean electricity

Solar and hydro already supply most of Malaysia's renewable output. Wind and biomass play smaller but growing roles.

Solar

Solar Energy

Panels convert sunlight straight into electricity with no moving parts. The most practical option for Malaysian rooftops.

Wind

Wind Energy

Moving air spins a turbine that drives a generator. Extremely cheap where the wind is strong and steady.

Hydro

Hydropower

Falling water turns a turbine. Malaysia's largest renewable source and the only one that can store energy at scale.

Biomass

Biomass

Plant waste is burned or converted to gas. Turns agricultural by-products into power instead of leaving them to rot.

    An honest view

    Benefits and challenges

    Renewable energy is not free of problems. Understanding the limitations is what separates informed support from slogans.

    Benefits

    • No fuel is burned during operation, so there are no carbon emissions from generation itself.
    • Sunlight, wind and water are not bought, so running costs stay low and predictable for decades.
    • Less dependence on imported fuel and on international price swings.
    • Systems can be small. A single household can generate its own power; a coal plant cannot be built one roof at a time.
    • Installation, maintenance and grid work create skilled local jobs.

    Challenges

    • Intermittency. Solar stops at night and drops under cloud. Supply has to match demand every second.
    • Storage cost. Batteries fix intermittency but are still expensive at grid scale.
    • Upfront investment. Cheap to run, but the installation cost arrives all at once.
    • Land and water use. Large solar farms and reservoirs occupy space that had another purpose.
    • End of life. Panels and blades need recycling routes that are still being developed.

    Solar Energy

    How it works

    A solar panel is made of silicon cells. When sunlight hits a cell it knocks electrons loose, and the cell's structure pushes those electrons in one direction. That flow is direct current. An inverter then converts it to the alternating current used by household appliances and the grid.

    Why it suits Malaysia

    Sunlight here is strong and consistent throughout the year, without the long dark winters that limit solar output in temperate countries. Panels have no moving parts, so maintenance is mostly keeping the surface clean.

    Main limitation

    Output stops completely at night and falls sharply under heavy cloud. Without a battery, a household still depends on the grid after dark, which is why solar is usually a bill reducer rather than a full replacement.

    Wind Energy

    How it works

    Turbine blades are shaped like aircraft wings. Moving air passing over them creates lift, which turns the rotor. The rotor drives a generator through a gearbox, and the generator produces electricity.

    Where it performs best

    Power rises steeply with wind speed, so a site with consistently strong wind produces far more than a site with occasional gusts. Coastlines, open plains and offshore locations are ideal.

    Main limitation

    Peninsular Malaysia has relatively low average wind speeds, which makes large-scale wind less attractive here than solar. Turbines also need spacing, so a wind farm covers a wide area even though the ground between towers can still be farmed.

    Hydropower

    How it works

    Water held behind a dam has potential energy because of its height. When it is released it falls through a turbine, and that movement drives a generator. The higher the drop and the greater the flow, the more electricity is produced.

    Its unique advantage

    Hydro is the only renewable source on this page that can be turned up or down on demand. Water can also be pumped back uphill when electricity is cheap and released when demand peaks, which makes a reservoir act as a very large battery.

    Main limitation

    Building a reservoir floods land, displaces communities and changes river ecosystems downstream. Extended dry periods also reduce output exactly when demand for cooling is highest.

    Biomass

    How it works

    Plant material is burned to boil water, and the steam drives a turbine. Alternatively the material can be broken down by bacteria in a sealed tank to produce biogas, which is then burned in an engine or gas turbine.

    Why it counts as renewable

    The carbon released was absorbed from the air by the plant while it grew, and the next crop absorbs it again. It is treated as renewable only when the material is genuinely replanted and not taken from cleared forest.

    Main limitation

    Burning still produces local air pollution, so exhaust treatment is essential. Supply also depends on agricultural waste being collected and transported, which adds cost and its own emissions.
