Electric vehicles

Fewer moving parts, a completely different fuel bill

An electric car replaces roughly two thousand moving engine parts with about twenty. That single change affects how it drives, what it costs to run, what can go wrong with it, and how clean it really is. This page works through each of those.

The basics

What is an electric vehicle?

An electric vehicle is any vehicle driven by one or more electric motors instead of, or alongside, a petrol or diesel engine. The energy comes from a rechargeable battery pack rather than a fuel tank.

The important difference is not just the fuel. A petrol engine converts only around a quarter of the energy in its fuel into movement; the rest leaves as heat and noise. An electric motor converts the large majority of the electricity it receives into movement. That efficiency gap is the reason an EV costs so much less per kilometre even where electricity is not cheap.

EVs are also mechanically simpler. There is no gearbox in most models, no exhaust system, no oil to change and no clutch to wear out, which reduces routine servicing considerably.

One number worth remembering

Energy use in an EV is measured in kilowatt-hours per 100 km, the same way a petrol car is measured in litres per 100 km. A typical compact EV uses somewhere around 15 to 18 kWh per 100 km.

Once you know that figure and your electricity tariff, working out the running cost is straightforward — which is exactly what the calculator further down this page does.

Types

Four types of electric vehicle

"EV" is often used as if it meant one thing. In practice there are four categories, and they differ in how much of the driving is actually electric. Save the ones you want to read about later.

BEV

Battery Electric

Runs on battery power only. No engine, no petrol, no exhaust. Must be plugged in to recharge. This is what most people mean by "an EV".

HEV

Hybrid Electric

A petrol engine works with a small battery and motor. The battery is charged by the engine and by braking, never from a plug. Fuel use drops, but it still runs on petrol.

PHEV

Plug-in Hybrid

A larger battery that can be charged from a plug, plus a petrol engine for longer trips. Short daily journeys can be fully electric while long drives remain possible.

FCEV

Fuel Cell Electric

Carries hydrogen and generates its own electricity on board. The only tailpipe output is water. Refuelling is fast, but hydrogen stations are still very rare.

    Under the floor

    How an EV works

    The steps below follow the energy from the wall socket to the wheels. Each stage loses a little energy as heat, but far less than a combustion engine loses.

    01

    Charging

    Electricity from the grid flows into the car. An on-board charger converts alternating current from the socket into the direct current the battery needs. Rapid chargers do this conversion outside the car, which is why they are faster.

    02

    Storage

    The battery pack sits low in the floor and holds the energy as chemical potential. Its position also lowers the car's centre of gravity, which improves stability in corners.

    03

    Conversion

    When the accelerator is pressed, an inverter converts the battery's direct current back into alternating current and controls exactly how much reaches the motor. This is what gives an EV such smooth, immediate response.

    04

    Drive and recovery

    The motor turns the wheels directly, with no gear changes in most models. When slowing down, the motor runs in reverse as a generator and sends energy back into the battery. This is regenerative braking, and it is why EVs are most efficient in stop-start city traffic rather than on highways.

    Benefits

    What EVs are genuinely good at

    No tailpipe emissions

    Nothing is emitted where the car is driven, which matters most in dense urban areas.

    Lower running cost

    Electricity per kilometre is usually far cheaper than petrol, and servicing is simpler.

    Much quieter

    Removing engine noise reduces one of the largest sources of noise pollution in cities.

    Gets cleaner over time

    As the grid adds renewables, every EV already on the road becomes cleaner without modification.

    Charging

    Three levels of charging

    Charging speed depends on how much power the charger can deliver. Faster is not always better: slow charging overnight is gentler on battery life and usually cheaper.

    The honest part

    Environmental impact

    EVs are cleaner overall, but the claim that they are "zero emission" is not accurate. Being able to explain why is more useful than repeating the slogan.

    Manufacturing costs more carbon

    Producing a battery pack is energy intensive, so an EV starts its life with a larger carbon debt than an equivalent petrol car.

    Driving pays that debt back

    Because it emits nothing while driving, the EV catches up over its lifetime and then moves clearly ahead. How quickly depends on how clean the local grid is.

    The grid matters

    An EV charged from coal-heavy electricity is much less clean than one charged from solar. This is why renewable energy and electric transport have to advance together.

    Batteries need a second life

    Mining lithium, nickel and cobalt has real environmental and social costs. Recycling and reusing old packs for grid storage is an active area of development.

    Side by side

    EV compared with a petrol vehicle

    Neither column is entirely better. Which one suits a driver depends mostly on where they park and how far they usually drive.

    Purchase price Usually higher at present Usually lower at present

    Reading the result carefully

    A large monthly difference does not automatically mean an EV is the cheaper choice. EVs usually cost more to buy, so the fuel saving has to run for several years before the total cost of ownership turns in their favour.

    The calculation is also sensitive to where you charge. Public fast charging can cost several times more per kWh than charging at home overnight, which changes the picture significantly.
